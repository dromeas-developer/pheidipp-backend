# Goal Modes

The same underlying data produces different coaching narratives depending on goal context. The coach does not apply a template — it reads the goal mode and speaks accordingly.

## Four Goal Modes

**Race / Goal event mode.** The plan is periodised and tailored toward a specific target date and performance goal. Every workout, phase, and recovery period serves the race. Coaching focuses on peaking, tapering, and race-specific preparation. The twin surfaces urgency where appropriate and frames each session as a step toward the goal event.

In race mode, plan generation begins with the training length gate — a critical first step that evaluates whether the goal timeline is appropriate before any hypothesis generation occurs. Goals beyond the planning horizon trigger an intermediate goal proposal focused on physiological foundations rather than a single long plan. Goals too close for the target distance with low fitness trigger a shorter goal proposal.

When the timeline is appropriate, the coach generates three distinct strategic approaches using four primary dimensions: training philosophy, progression pattern, recovery structure, and intensity balance. Each approach represents a genuinely different coaching philosophy. The coach selects the best approach and produces a phase arc — a week-by-week description of what the training is about, with the methodology, physiological emphasis, and intensity character for each week. See plan-generation for the full generation process.

**Fitness Improvement mode.** Active development phase focused on measurable physiological gains and capacity expansion. The plan uses progressive overload with structured adaptation blocks. Coaching emphasizes development milestones, strength building, and measurable progress. The twin tracks adaptation signals and surfaces improvement patterns.

The phase arc is not a generic template — it is constructed from the athlete's seeded objectives. Each block (6–12 weeks, computed from objectives and confidence level) is divided into phase groups that address specific objective clusters. The block structure adapts to the athlete's profile: low confidence triggers longer base phases for data accumulation; high confidence enables faster progression. Block N+1 begins with adjusted objectives based on Block N outcomes, creating a rolling development cycle that responds to actual progress.

Coaching in fitness improvement mode frames each session in terms of the objective it serves. Pre-workout messages connect the session to the relevant development area. Post-workout messages report progress against that objective. Phase transitions are acknowledged explicitly — the coach explains what has been achieved and what the next phase will develop. When all objectives in a block are achieved early, new objectives are seeded immediately and the next block begins without waiting for the calendar to elapse.

The plan view presents the block structure with phase groups, active objectives, and development focus:

```
Phase 1: Base (weeks 1-4)
  Objectives: Aerobic base quality, Structural tolerance
  Focus: Building your aerobic foundation and structural resilience

Phase 2: Build (weeks 5-7)
  Objectives: Threshold quality, Pacing discipline
  Focus: Developing threshold capacity and pacing consistency

Phase 3: Integration (week 8)
  Objectives: All active objectives
  Focus: Consolidating gains across all development areas
```

Daily view pre-workout messages connect the session to the specific objective it serves:

- "Today's session serves your threshold quality objective — 4x8min at LT2 to develop your threshold capacity"

Phase transition messages acknowledge what was achieved and frame the next phase:

- "Your aerobic base objective is progressing well. We're now shifting focus to your threshold quality objective."

**Maintenance mode.** Consistency-focused relationship for athletes not pursuing specific goals. The coaching posture shifts away from periodisation toward habit preservation and gradual, sustainable improvement. Success is defined as consistent training, fitness preservation, and injury-free consistency. The twin maintains current fitness while warning against over-adaptation.

The phase arc uses a minimal rolling 4-week block structure. Weeks 1–3 emphasise consistent aerobic training at easy intensity (85–90% of volume). Week 4 is a recovery and consolidation week with reduced volume (60–70% of weeks 1–3). The block repeats with minor session type variation, creating a sustainable rhythm without periodisation pressure. Hard training is capped at 5–10% of weekly volume — enough to preserve capacity, never enough to drive adaptation.

Checkpoints in maintenance mode are infrequent and non-disruptive. A benchmark session every 8–12 weeks confirms the athlete is holding their current level. Progress reviews every 4 weeks align with block completion. Calibration checkpoints fire only if confidence drops, such as after an extended break. The coaching message is reassuring, not demanding: fitness is being preserved, not chased.

Consistency is measured explicitly. Session completion rate, weekly volume stability, recovery week compliance, and consecutive completed blocks are tracked. When consistency exceeds 90% over 3+ blocks and confidence has improved, the coach proposes transitioning to fitness improvement mode. The transition is framed as recognition of readiness: "Your consistency has been excellent. You have demonstrated capacity for progressive development."

Coaching messages in maintenance mode reinforce sustainability and pattern recognition:

```
Maintenance Block (weeks 1-4)
  Objectives: Maintain aerobic base, Preserve training consistency
  Focus: Consistent training with sustainable habits
```

Daily view messages acknowledge the value of consistency:

- "You've been consistent for 6 weeks. Your fitness is stable. This week maintains your current load with a recovery week to keep things sustainable."

Block completion messages reinforce the pattern:

- "Another consistent block. Your fitness is holding steady. Let's continue with the same approach."

**Recovery mode.** Healing-focused phase following injury, illness, or deliberate deload. The plan uses conservative load progression with structural healing priority. Coaching is protective and monitoring, emphasising gradual return to normal training. The twin reduces load recommendations and prioritises recovery indicators over progression.

The phase arc is severity-driven. Three phases progress from minimal load through gradual return to transition: Phase 1 keeps volume at 30–50% of pre-injury baseline with easy-only intensity and 3–4 sessions per week. Phase 2 gradually increases volume (50–75% of baseline) and introduces moderate effort in the second half if healing markers are stable. Phase 3 reaches 75–90% of baseline, resumes normal session types, and includes a calibration checkpoint to verify threshold estimates before proposing mode transition.

Phase transitions are gated by healing assessment, not calendar time. The athlete advances from Phase 1 to Phase 2 only when wellness signals have returned to their own baseline for 7+ consecutive days. Phase 2 to Phase 3 requires confirmed volume tolerance and 5+ consecutive green recovery days. Setbacks trigger immediate response: wellness degradation extends the current phase and reduces load; major setbacks return to Phase 1.

Checkpoints in recovery mode are healing-focused. Progress reviews at each phase transition assess whether the athlete is ready to increase load. A calibration checkpoint at Phase 3 verifies threshold estimates after the recovery period. The coaching message is protective: "Healing assessment — checking whether you're ready to increase load."

When recovery is complete, the target mode depends on confidence. Low confidence transitions to maintenance for consistency before development. Medium or high confidence transitions directly to fitness improvement. The transition is framed as recognition of healing: "Recovery complete. You are ready for progressive development."

Coaching messages in recovery mode are protective and monitoring-focused:

```
Recovery Block (weeks 1-4)
  Phase 1: Minimal Load (weeks 1-2) — Healing and monitoring
  Phase 2: Gradual Return (weeks 3-4) — Progressive load increase
  Phase 3: Transition (week 5) — Resuming normal training
```

Daily view messages acknowledge the healing context:

- Phase 1: "You're in week 2 of your return. We're keeping load minimal while your body heals."
- Phase 2: "Your wellness signals are back to baseline. We'll start introducing moderate effort this week."
- Phase 3: "You've completed the recovery phase. Let's verify your thresholds and transition to progressive development."

Setback messages maintain the protective stance:

- "Your wellness signals have dipped this week. Let's maintain the current load and monitor closely."

## Adaptive Coaching Language

In race mode: "sharpening," "final prep," "race-specific," urgency where it serves. In fitness improvement mode: "development," "progressive overload," "capacity building," measurable gains. In maintenance mode: "consistency," "gradual progress," "sustainable habits," patience where the athlete might otherwise feel pressure. In recovery mode: "healing," "protective," "gradual return," monitoring where the athlete might otherwise push too hard.

The athlete should never have to tell the coach which mode they're in day to day — the coach already knows and speaks from that context.

## Goal Transitions

When an athlete completes a goal event, the coach acknowledges it and initiates a natural conversation about what comes next — recovery period, new goal, or open mode. This transition is a coaching moment, not a form to fill in. The coach frames it as part of the ongoing relationship.

When an athlete sets a new goal event mid-cycle, the plan restructures accordingly. The twin reassesses current fitness and fatigue state and rebuilds the periodisation toward the new target. The coach communicates the logic of the new structure.

**Mode Switching:**

- From Race to any other: Coach initiates recovery period first, then transitions to chosen mode based on athlete's readiness and goals.
- From Recovery to Fitness Improvement: Coach monitors healing markers before recommending progression.
- From Maintenance to Fitness Improvement: Coach detects readiness signals and proposes development phase.
- From any mode to Maintenance: Natural fallback when athlete wants consistency without specific targets.

Transitions are always framed as coaching conversations, not administrative changes. The twin provides the rationale while the coach delivers it in the appropriate voice for both the current and upcoming mode.

## What Never Changes Between Modes

The quality of analysis, the coach's voice, and the twin's underlying physiological modelling are identical in all modes. Non-race modes are not stripped-down experiences — they are genuinely different coaching relationships for athletes pursuing goals beyond racing. The only thing that changes is what the coaching is oriented toward.
